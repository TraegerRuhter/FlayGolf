## 1. FTC advertising law: substantiation, puffery, health claims, endorsements/reviews, Made in USA, guarantees

### Takeaway
Any objective claim Flay makes, including one inside a joke, needs a "reasonable basis" before it runs. Health or body-benefit claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence, usually randomized controlled human trials. Vague superiority talk ("puffery") is safe; anything measurable is not. Since 2024, fake or misleading reviews and testimonials can bring civil penalties for a first violation. Unqualified "Made in USA" claims need "all or virtually all" US content and also carry civil penalties. Guarantees must state their conditions.

### Cited Findings
**Substantiation doctrine**
- The FTC held in 1972 that "it is an unfair practice in violation of the [FTC] Act to make an affirmative product claim without a reasonable basis for making that claim" (In re Pfizer, 81 F.T.C. 23, 62 (1972)). — [Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Advertising_FTC_Reasonable_Basis_Standard.pdf); [BBB National Programs blog](https://bbbprograms.org/media/insights/blog/pivotal-moments-for-ad-law-in-the-70s)
- The "Pfizer factors" set how much substantiation a claim needs: type of claim, type of product, consequences of a false claim, benefit of a truthful claim, cost and ease of developing substantiation, and the amount of substantiation experts consider reasonable. Sources count them as five or six depending on grouping. — [Davis+Gilbert](https://dglaw.com/press-alert-details.cfm?id=906); [FTC Commissioner Wilson statement, 2020](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/1568183/2020-02-28_commissioner_wilson_statement_final.pdf)
- The 1984 Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation (49 Fed. Reg. 30999) restated these factors. If an ad expressly or impliedly claims a level of support (for example "tests show" or "lab-proven"), the advertiser must have at least that level. **[UNVERIFIED wording; the "express level of substantiation" point is standard doctrine but I did not fetch the primary text]** — [Davis Wright Tremaine, 2024](https://dwt.com/insights/2024/03/how-to-substantiate-advertising-claims)

**Health claims and the 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance**
- The FTC issued the Health Products Compliance Guidance on December 20, 2022, replacing the 1998 dietary supplement guide. It covers all health-related products, including foods, OTC drugs, homeopathic products, health equipment, diagnostic tests and apps. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide); [Jones Day](https://m.jonesday.com/en/insights/2023/01/ftc-overhauls-and-expands-25yearold-health-products-advertising-compliance-guide)
- Under that guidance, health claims generally need randomized, controlled human clinical testing. Animal and in-vitro studies, anecdotes and testimonials generally do not count as competent and reliable scientific evidence. Testimonials cannot substitute for science, and a disclaimer cannot cure some claims. The guidance does not have the force of law. — [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/after-almost-25-years-ftc-issues-new-health-products-compliance-guidance/); [JD Supra summary](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/ftc-releases-health-products-compliance-2240320)
- The FTC's 2016 Enforcement Policy Statement on OTC homeopathic drugs holds homeopathic efficacy claims to the same standard as other drugs. It found that for most such products "the case for efficacy is based solely on traditional homeopathic theories and there are no valid studies using current scientific methods." It also warns that an ad conveying more substantiation than the marketer has violates the FTC Act. — [FTC policy statement PDF](https://Www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/996984/p114505_otc_homeopathic_drug_enforcement_policy_statement.pdf); [FTC press release](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/11/ftc-issues-enforcement-policy-statement-regarding-marketing); [Federal Register, Dec 13, 2016](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-12-13/html/2016-29770.htm)

**Puffery vs. measurable claims**
- In *Pizza Hut v. Papa John's*, 227 F.3d 489 (5th Cir. 2000), the court held "Better Ingredients. Better Pizza." on its own to be non-actionable puffery, meaning a general claim of superiority. It treated the slogan differently when paired with specific, quantifiable comparisons. — [Justia opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/227/489/615120/); [5th Cir. PDF](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/pub/00/00-10071.cv0.wpd.pdf); [Venable puffery overview](https://www.venable.com/files/Publication/073d0951-9fa6-4977-9e68-4deb21a819d8/Presentation/PublicationAttachment/c245d881-6fd8-434e-b068-52959159e864/Best-Explanation-and-Update-on-Puffery-You-Will-Ever-Read-Antitrust-Summer-2017.pdf)

**Endorsement Guides (2023) and the Fake Reviews Rule (2024)**
- The revised Endorsement Guides (16 C.F.R. Part 255; 88 Fed. Reg. 48092) took effect July 26, 2023. They removed the "results not typical" safe harbor: atypical testimonials need proof that the results are typical, or a clear disclosure of generally expected results. Experts must actually have the expertise they are presented as having. — [BakerHostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/ftc-revises-endorsement-and-testimonial-guides); [Kelley Drye FAQ summary](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/client-advisories/federal-trade-commission-addresses-frequently-asked-questions-about-the-revised-endorsement-guides)
- The FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials was finalized in August 2024 and took effect October 21, 2024. It bans:
  - fake or false reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones;
  - buying positive or negative reviews;
  - insider reviews that don't disclose the reviewer's connection to the business;
  - review suppression through threats;
  - fake social-media influence indicators.
  — [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)
- Violations can bring civil penalties, reported at $51,744 per violation (2024 inflation figure, likely adjusted since), against violators with actual or implied knowledge. — [Davis Wright Tremaine](https://dwt.com/insights/2024/08/ftc-finalizes-rule-banning-fake-consumer-reviews); [Kelley Drye](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ftc-finalizes-rule-on-fake-reviews)

**Made in USA**
- The Made in USA Labeling Rule (finalized 2021) requires unqualified claims to be "all or virtually all" US-made. Final assembly or processing and all significant processing must happen in the US, and all or virtually all components must be US-made and US-sourced. It covers labels and online or mail-order marketing that shows a Made in USA seal or mark. It allows civil penalties (up to $43,280 per violation at adoption; adjusted for inflation since). — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/advertising-law/ftc-finalizes-made-in-usa-labeling-rule); [MSU Extension](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/ftc-s-new-made-in-the-usa-rule)
- One law firm counts 11 enforcement actions and more than $15 million in judgments since the rule was finalized. — [AFS Law](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/consumer-products-watch/ftc-continues-target-misleading-made-usa-claims)

**Guarantees and warranties**
- The FTC Guides for the Advertising of Warranties and Guarantees (16 C.F.R. Part 239) apply to advertising of any warranty or guarantee. §239.3 covers "satisfaction guarantees" and similar representations; one example conditions a guarantee on returning the unused portion within 30 days. I did not retrieve the exact §239.3 text: it reportedly requires material conditions to be disclosed and refunds to be paid as promised. **[UNVERIFIED wording]** — [eCFR Part 239](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-239); [GovInfo 2024 §239.4](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2024-title16-vol1/pdf/CFR-2024-title16-vol1-sec239-4.pdf)

**Fake scarcity / dark patterns**
- The FTC staff report *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light* (Sept 2022) names false scarcity claims ("Only two left in stock" when stock is plentiful) and fake countdown timers as deceptive design. True urgency or scarcity messages are not necessarily dark patterns. — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-releases-report-on-dark-patterns); [Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/blogs/2022/09/the-ftc-brings-more-light-to-dark-patterns-in-new)

### Inferences
- A joke can still make a claim. The FTC judges the "net impression" (homeopathy statement). A parody "remedy" ad that says "Adds 40 yards!" as a gag could still be read literally by some consumers, especially in a short clip shown out of context. The punchline has to be obviously impossible, or immediately undercut, inside the same frame.
- Flay's own claims should be ones it can measure: dimensions, materials, print process, compatibility with named sim setups, durability under a stated test. Anything about swing, distance, scoring, back health or posture moves toward performance or health substantiation. Health claims carry the RCT expectation, which a tiny brand cannot meet, so avoid them entirely.
- "Tested" or "lab-tested" language raises the bar to whatever testing the wording implies.
- If Flay marks 3D-printed goods "Made in USA," the filament and other components must also qualify. Qualified versions such as "Printed in [State] from imported filament" are safer. **[LAWYER if unsure]**
- Apparel is generally also subject to the Textile Act and its country-of-origin labeling. I did not research this; see Gaps.

## 2. Lanham Act false advertising, comparative advertising, and NAD challenges (golf examples)

### Takeaway
Competitors, and even non-competitors with commercial injury, can sue under Lanham Act §43(a) over false or misleading statements about their products. Saying or implying that a named or identifiable competitor's product "doesn't work" is a factual claim Flay would have to prove. In golf, big OEMs actively use the NAD against each other's distance claims.

### Cited Findings
- *Lexmark v. Static Control* (S. Ct. 2014, unanimous) gives standing to plaintiffs whose injury falls within the Lanham Act's zone of interests (commercial interest in reputation or sales) and is proximately caused by the deception. Direct competition is not required, and disparaging a plaintiff's products to customers supported proximate cause. — [Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-873); [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/alert/supreme-court-resolves-circuit-split-on-standing-in-lanham-act-false-advertising-cases)
- Golf NAD history:
  - In June 2013 the NAD decided two golf-club cases brought by TaylorMade and Callaway. One examined whether ROCKETBALLZ ads implied the club would add 17 yards for the average golfer regardless of skill. — [BBB National Programs](https://bbbprograms.org/media/newsroom/decisions/nad-reviews-advertising-claims-for-golf-clubs-following-challenges-by-taylor-made-callaway)
  - TaylorMade reportedly dropped "average golfer" from its ads after the challenge. — [Truth in Advertising](https://truthinadvertising.org/articles/taylormade-rocketballz-fairway-woods)
  - Callaway was on the losing side of an NAD decision over "Longest Driver in Golf" for the RAZR Fit Xtreme. — [MyGolfSpy](https://mygolfspy.com/taylormade-callaway-nad-marketing-claims/); [Truth in Advertising](https://www.truthinadvertising.org/callaway-razr-fit-xtreme-driver/)
  - MyGolfSpy also covered Callaway's "2 clubs longer" claim being found misleading. — [MyGolfSpy](https://mygolfspy.com/news-opinion/callaways-2-clubs-longer-claim-is-misleading/)
  - A separate NAD matter found Bridgestone Golf could support certain claims challenged by Acushnet (date not confirmed). — [Hogwire](https://hogwire.biz/?p=219)
  - Callaway v. Dunlop (early 2000s) was a court false-advertising case over "longest ball on the pro tour." — [search result summary; Golf Business News](https://www.golfbusinessnews.com/news/corporate/spalding-back-in-court/)
- The NAD is a self-regulatory forum: it "monitors the marketplace, holds advertisers responsible for their claims." — [Sports Litigation Alert](https://sportslitigationalert.com/can-we-get-a-mulligan/)

### Inferences
- The golf industry's own record supports Flay's satirical thesis. Distance and "average golfer" claims have been challenged, and Flay can cite these public decisions accurately as commentary. Describe them exactly as decided; don't exaggerate the outcomes.
- Comparative tests of competitor products, even mocking ones, should be accurate and reproducible, and should disclose methodology (see Section 7). A "we tested X and it did nothing" claim invites a Lanham Act or NAD challenge if the test is weak.
- A tiny brand is unlikely to be an NAD target, but a large OEM can afford a cease-and-desist letter or a lawsuit. The cost of defending one is the real risk. **[LAWYER before naming any brand in a comparative claim]**

## 3. Parody and satire defenses: trademark parody, defamation, trade libel

### Takeaway
Parody that comments on someone else's mark without using it as Flay's own brand still gets meaningful protection. After *Jack Daniel's v. VIP* (2023), a parody used as Flay's product name, logo or trade dress gets no First Amendment (Rogers) shortcut and is judged on ordinary likelihood of confusion. For defamation and trade libel, satire that cannot reasonably be read as stating actual facts is protected. False factual claims about a real company's product are not.

### Cited Findings
- *Jack Daniel's Properties v. VIP Products*, No. 22-148 (S. Ct. June 8, 2023, unanimous):
  - The Rogers test does not apply when the alleged infringer uses a mark as a designation of source for its own goods. Such a claim "rises or falls on likelihood of confusion," though parody can still bear on that analysis.
  - The dilution "noncommercial use" exclusion does not shield parody that is itself source-identifying.
  - Commentators call the holding narrow.
  — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2023/06/a-win-for-trademark-owners-the-supreme-courts-ruling-in-jack-daniels-properties-inc-v-vip-products); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/supreme-court-clarifies-limits-of-first-amendment-defenses-to-use-of-trademarks-in-parody-products/); [Akerman](https://www.akerman.com/en/perspectives/ip-not-funny-unanimous-scotus-in-jack-daniels-v-vip-holds-that-parody-does-not-implicate-first-amendment-concerns-but-only-implicates-likelihood-of-confusion.html)
- *Louis Vuitton Malletier v. Haute Diggity Dog*, 507 F.3d 252 (4th Cir. 2007): "Chewy Vuiton" dog toys were a successful parody, with no likelihood of confusion and no dilution. Because the parody was obvious, it weighed against confusion. — [Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca4/06-2267/062267.p-2011-03-14.html); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/publications/2007/11/20071101_fourth_circuit_chewy_vuiton_dec_2174)
- *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988): the jury found the ad parody could not "reasonably be understood as describing actual facts," which defeated libel. A public figure cannot recover for emotional distress over parody without a false statement of fact made with actual malice. — [Columbia Global Freedom of Expression](https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/?p=44048); [vLex opinion text](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/485-u-s-46-605081002)
- Trade libel / product disparagement generally requires publication of a false statement of fact about the plaintiff's product, made with malice (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard), causing special (pecuniary) damages. Opinions are not actionable, but an implied factual assertion without disclosed supporting facts can be. — [CACI No. 1731 (California)](https://justia.com/trials-litigation/docs/caci/1700/1731); [HipSaver v. Kiel (Mass. 2013)](https://law.justia.com/cases/massachusetts/supreme-court/2013/sjc-11085.html); [Stimmel Law](https://stimmel-law.com/articles/trade-libel-elements-cause-action-and-defenses-available/)

### Inferences
- **Mock categories and tactics, not small named companies.** Targets like "the miracle swing gadget," "the 20-yards-in-a-box driver ad," "the infomercial testimonial" are commentary on a genre with no identifiable plaintiff. Naming a small company is riskier: it's more likely to read as a factual accusation, more likely to be private (no actual-malice protection), and more likely to feel personal enough to sue.
- When referencing big OEMs, stick to public record (for example "NAD found X claim unsupported in 2013") and clearly labeled opinion ("we think distance marketing is a circus").
- **Never use a real competitor's mark, or a confusingly similar spoof of it, as Flay's own product name, logo or label.** After *Jack Daniel's* that's the highest-risk parody move. "Chewy Vuiton"-style punning on a real brand as a product name now goes through a full confusion analysis. **[LAWYER for any product name that riffs on a real mark]**
- The satire needs to be obvious on its face (absurd claims, a "parody" label, Flay branding everywhere). That helps both the trademark-confusion argument and the "not reasonably understood as fact" defense.

## 4. Parody "medicine" products: FDA intended-use risk and keeping novelties clearly novelty

### Takeaway
Under the FD&C Act, a product's regulatory status follows its intended use, which FDA infers from labeling, advertising and claims, express or implied. A bottle styled as a "remedy" that claims to cure or treat anything (including "golf elbow" or back pain) risks being treated as an unapproved, misbranded drug, whatever the joke. Flay's novelty items should make no health or body claims, contain nothing ingestible or topical, and say so plainly.

### Cited Findings
- FDA amended its intended-use regulations (21 C.F.R. 201.128 for drugs; 801.4 for devices) in a final rule published August 2, 2021. Labeling, promotional claims and advertising are key evidence of intended use. Implied claims are relevant, and a firm's stated subjective intent is not necessarily determinative. — [Federal Register, Aug 2, 2021](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2021-08-02/pdf/2021-15980.pdf); [McDermott](https://www.mwe.com/?p=16241); [21 C.F.R. 201.128 text](https://syfert.com/cfr/sections/21-201.128.html)
- A food or other product intended to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat or prevent disease becomes a drug unless it fits an authorized health claim. — [FDLI presentation](https://www.fdli.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Davidson-Jennifer.pdf)
- The FTC applies drug-level substantiation standards to homeopathic-style efficacy claims and judges the net impression (Section 1). — [FTC homeopathy statement](https://Www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/996984/p114505_otc_homeopathic_drug_enforcement_policy_statement.pdf)

### Inferences
These are my reasoning from the intended-use framework. I found no FDA guidance specifically on parody or novelty "remedy" props. **[LAWYER before launching any remedy-styled SKU]**
- Safest formats:
  - empty apothecary bottles, or bottles holding obviously non-consumable items (golf tees, ball markers, a rolled-up "prescription" scorecard);
  - stickers, posters and apparel showing fake labels;
  - labels that say "Contains: nothing. Cures: nothing. Not for consumption."
- Riskier formats:
  - **Candy, mints, drinks or "elixirs":** they become food and trigger FDA food labeling, allergen and facility rules. Any "cures your slice" wording on a food invites intended-use questions.
  - **Lotions, balms, rubs:** cosmetic or drug status turns on claims; "soothes golfer's elbow" is a drug claim.
  - **Supplements of any kind.** Avoid.
- Don't let the parody name a real disease or body condition with a treatment verb (cure, relieve, heal, fix, treat, prevent), even jokingly. Keep the "ailments" fictional and golf-only ("Chronic Three-Putt Syndrome," "Acute Shankitis"), and add a visible "not a real condition / not medicine" line.
- Keep the marketing consistent. Intended use is read from all promotion, so an off-hand TikTok caption saying "actually helps my back" could undermine the novelty position.
- State law adds exposure. State UDAP ("little FTC Act") statutes, and state food/drug laws such as California's Sherman Law, can reach the same conduct. **[UNVERIFIED; not researched in depth]**

- I found no FDA guidance or enforcement history specifically about novelty or parody "medicine" products, or "placebo" gag gifts. This would need targeted legal research.
- I did not research child-safety or CPSC issues for glass apothecary bottles.

## 5. Historical patent-medicine ads and labels: public domain vs. live trademarks

### Takeaway
Old ad artwork may be out of copyright, but brand names can still be live trademarks: copyright and trademark are separate. Lydia Pinkham marks appear to still be registered and used commercially. Dr. Bronner's is an active, litigious brand. Flay should use generic period styling and its own invented names, not reproduce real historical brands.

### Cited Findings
- The LYDIA E. PINKHAM mark (Reg. No. 0633056) shows status "Registered and Renewed" with a 2015 renewal. Related filings list Numark Laboratories as owner, and Lydia Pinkham Herbal Compound/Supplement is still sold by Numark. Ownership records on aggregators conflict, so check USPTO TSDR. — [Justia trademark record](https://trademarks.justia.com/716/95/lydia-e-71695991.html); [Justia Numark owner page](https://trademarks.justia.com/owners/numark-laboratories-inc-3314); [Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Lydia_Pinkham)
- Dr. Bronner's is an active brand that updated its Magic Soap labels in March 2025 and has sued competitors over "organic" claims. One "DR.BRONNER'S & SUN DOG'S MAGIC" registration was cancelled in 2016 under Section 8, but this does not mean its other marks or trade dress are unprotected. — [Dr. Bronner's press release](https://info.drbronner.com/media-center/press-release/dr-bronners-updates-product-labels-on-its-magic-soap-for-2025/); [Justia record](https://trademark.justia.com/785/90/dr-bronner-s-sun-dog-s-78590754.html); [San Diego Business Journal](https://www.sdbj.com/?p=19576)
- *Jack Daniel's* (Section 3) means a spoof of a real label used as Flay's own trade dress is judged on likelihood of confusion. — [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/supreme-court-clarifies-limits-of-first-amendment-defenses-to-use-of-trademarks-in-parody-products/)

### Inferences
- US works published before 1931 are generally in the public domain as of 2026 (copyright term for 1930 works expired January 1, 2026). This is general copyright knowledge, not sourced here. **[UNVERIFIED; LAWYER for specific images]** Even so, a modern reproduction or restoration can carry its own rights, and museum or archive images often come with license terms.
- Safest approach: draw original artwork "in the style of" Victorian patent-medicine ads (fonts, borders, engraving look), with invented names ("Professor Flay's Patented Fairway Tonic — Contains Nothing"). Don't scan and reuse real labels from Pinkham, Bronner's, Carter's, Hamlin's Wizard Oil, Clark Stanley's Snake Oil and the like, without a TSDR clearance search.
- Run a USPTO search on every invented name before printing.

## 6. Platform ad policies: Meta, Google, TikTok

### Takeaway
All three platforms judge ads largely at face value, so a satirical "miracle cure" ad can be rejected or get the account flagged. Google explicitly prohibits making an improbable result look likely. TikTok prohibits promising or exaggerating results and restricts unsubstantiated absolute terms. Meta restricts misleading health claims and before-and-after imagery in health and weight-loss contexts.

### Cited Findings
- Google Ads' "Unreliable claims" policy, under Misrepresentation, covers inaccurate claims and claims "that make an improbable result look like the likely outcome, even if this result is possible." YouTube and Discover add restrictions on exaggerated claims and unrealistic transformation promises in health ads. — [Google Ads Unreliable claims](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/15936857); [Google false/misleading/unrealistic claims](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6086777); [YouTube ad requirements](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/10249050/ad-requirements-for-youtube)
- TikTok's "Misleading and false content" ad policy: ads and landing pages must not promise or exaggerate a product's results. Absolute terms ("Number 1") are restricted. Comparisons are allowed with supporting evidence or a clear disclaimer. — [TikTok Ads policy](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-misleading-and-false-content)
- Meta's Advertising Standards include an "Unacceptable Business Practices" policy under Fraud and Scams. Secondary guides report that before-and-after imagery is restricted for weight-loss products, and that misleading health claims violate Meta standards. I could not retrieve Meta's current wording directly. — [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/fraud-scams/unacceptable-business-practices/); [Accelerated Digital Media 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2025/) **[UNVERIFIED specifics]**

### Inferences
- Satire is far more likely to survive as organic content than as paid ads. For paid ads, lead with the honest claim and make the parody visual. Don't put a fake miracle claim in the headline, thumbnail or first second of video, where a reviewer or algorithm sees it without context.
- Avoid fake before-and-after split screens, even jokey ones, in paid placements.
- Repeated rejections can restrict the account, so test risky creative organically first.

- I didn't fetch Meta's exact current Health & Wellness policy text, or TikTok's policy on satire or parody specifically. No platform appears to have a published "satire exemption" for ads (not confirmed).

## 7. Honest-claims practices, do/don't checklist, and publishable "Honest Claims" framework

### Takeaway
Flay's legal posture and its brand promise point the same way: claim only what can be measured, publish how it was measured, state what the product won't do, and make the jokes impossible to take literally. The checklist and framework below turn Sections 1–6 into operating rules. They are inferences built on the cited law above, not legal requirements in themselves.

### Cited Findings
(Sources supporting the rules below, already cited above.)
- Reasonable basis before claims; "tests show" claims need that level of testing — [Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Advertising_FTC_Reasonable_Basis_Standard.pdf)
- Atypical testimonials need typical-results disclosure — [BakerHostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/ftc-revises-endorsement-and-testimonial-guides)
- No fake, bought or undisclosed-insider reviews — [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials)
- No false scarcity or fake timers — [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-releases-report-on-dark-patterns)
- Guarantee terms disclosed — [eCFR Part 239](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-239)
- Made in USA "all or virtually all" — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/advertising-law/ftc-finalizes-made-in-usa-labeling-rule)

### Inferences

#### Do / Don't checklist (32 items)
**DO**
1. Write down substantiation (test, spec sheet, photo, measurement) for every objective claim *before* it's published, and keep it on file.
2. Limit product claims to measurable facts: dimensions, weight, material, print method, compatible mats or sims, colorways, care instructions.
3. Publish a short test protocol and the raw results whenever you say "tested," and match the wording to the test ("survived 500 strikes on our mat" — not "indestructible").
4. Include a "What it won't do" line on every product page (for example "Won't add distance. Won't fix your slice. Won't lower your handicap.").
5. Make every parody claim absurd on its face and undercut in the same frame ("Cures 100% of shanks*" with "*It does not. Nothing does." in equal prominence).
6. Label parody products and ads with a visible "PARODY / NOVELTY — NOT MEDICINE" mark.
7. Mock categories, tropes and tactics ("the infomercial guarantee," "the miracle swing gizmo") rather than named small companies.
8. When you reference a big brand, stick to accurate public record (such as NAD outcomes), link the source, and label opinion as opinion.
9. Use invented brand names and original "in the style of" artwork for apothecary and patent-medicine designs.
10. Clear every new product name and parody brand through a USPTO search (and ideally an attorney) before printing.
11. Keep novelty "remedy" items non-ingestible and non-topical: empty bottles, bottles of tees, stickers, posters, shirts.
12. Put "Contains nothing. Treats nothing. Do not consume." on any bottle-style item.
13. Disclose any connection when using customer content: gifted product, paid, employee, friend or affiliate.
14. Show real reviews unfiltered, including negative ones, and never gate reviews by rating.
15. State guarantee and return terms in full next to the guarantee claim: window, condition, who pays shipping, refund method. Then honor them.
16. Qualify origin claims precisely ("3D-printed in [State], USA, from imported PLA") unless "all or virtually all" is true.
17. When testing competitor products, buy them at retail, disclose that you bought them, publish the method, and give each product the same conditions.
18. Run scarcity or urgency messaging only when it's literally true (real drop sizes, real end dates).
19. Get a short ad-law review of the launch campaign, product names and any remedy-styled SKU from an attorney. **[LAWYER]**
20. For paid ads, lead with the honest claim and keep parody visual. Test edgy creative organically first.

**DON'T**
21. Don't make any health, injury, pain, posture, or medical claim about anything Flay sells, as a joke or otherwise.
22. Don't use treatment verbs (cure, heal, relieve, treat, prevent) next to real body conditions, even in parody.
23. Don't sell ingestible "elixirs," candy "pills," balms or lotions as part of the parody line without separate regulatory advice.
24. Don't use a real company's trademark, or a confusingly close spoof, as the name, logo or label of a Flay product (*Jack Daniel's* risk).
25. Don't reproduce real historical patent-medicine brand names or labels (for example Lydia Pinkham) without a clearance check, even if the artwork is old.
26. Don't say or imply that a named or identifiable competitor's product "doesn't work" unless you have rigorous, documented proof.
27. Don't make distance, speed, handicap or "strokes saved" claims for Flay's own products.
28. Don't post fake testimonials in a "real" format (realistic names and photos presented as customers), even in a parody infomercial. Use obviously fictional characters, labeled as such.
29. Don't buy reviews, write reviews yourself, use AI-generated reviews, or suppress negative ones.
30. Don't run fake countdown timers, false "only 3 left," or fake "price slashed" anchors.
31. Don't put "Made in USA" on items with significant imported components without qualification.
32. Don't use fake before-and-after imagery or "results not typical" disclaimers in paid ads.

#### Draft publishable "Flay Honest Claims" framework
1. **We only claim what we can show you.** Every factual statement about a Flay product is something we've measured, and we link how.
2. **Open protocols, raw numbers.** When we say "tested," we publish the protocol (equipment, conditions, sample size, who ran it) and the raw data. Nothing gets rounded up into a superlative.
3. **The "won't do" list.** Every product page says what the product won't do, because golf has enough promises.
4. **Jokes are jokes.** Our parody remedies, infomercials and apothecary labels are satire. They contain nothing, cure nothing, and are labeled that way. If a joke could be mistaken for a real claim, we rewrite it.
5. **We aim at tactics, not people.** We poke fun at industry marketing habits, not small businesses. When we cite a real ruling about a real brand, we link the source.
6. **Fair fights.** If we test someone else's product, we buy it ourselves, test it the same way as ours, publish the method, and invite corrections.
7. **Real reviews only.** We don't buy, write or filter reviews. Gifted or paid content is disclosed.
8. **No fake urgency.** If we say a drop is limited, we say how many we made. If a sale ends, it actually ends.
9. **Plain-English guarantee.** [Insert actual terms: window, condition, refund method, who pays shipping.] We list exceptions in the same place as the promise.
10. **Where it's made, exactly.** We say where it's printed or sewn and where the materials come from.
11. **Corrections log.** If we get a claim wrong, we fix it publicly and date the change.
